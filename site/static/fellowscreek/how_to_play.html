How to Play

Fellows Creek

Build your life along the creek — and help everyone prosper.

Fellows Creek runs from the forest to the coast and is essential salmon habitat. After years of resource extraction and a changing climate, the creek needs care. Pick a role, work the land, and decide — together — how to share the water.

1 Your goal
Help the whole watershed thrive — not just your own patch.

Each of you has a role with its own needs, and you each want to do well. But the creek is one connected system: what you do upstream lands on whoever is downstream. You win together when the community and the creek are both healthy at the end of the game. The hard part is that the cheap, easy choice for you is often the costly one for someone else.

2 Choose your role

Four players, four jobs along the creek. Each role sees the watershed differently, has its own tools, and brings one special skill no other player can use.

Farmer
Grows crops and needs to draw water from the creek to irrigate fields. Can build water infrastructure like channels and storage ponds.
reliable water for the fields
Special skill
Build a storage pond
Only the Farmer can bank wet-season water in a pond — easing the dry-season choice between irrigating and leaving enough flow for fish. The stored water can even be shared as a bargaining chip.
Ecologist
Restores the creek's health. Wants big logs left in the water, healthy plant buffers along the banks, and enough flow for salmon to pass.
fish & wildlife to recover
Special skill
Anchor logs to the bank
Only the Ecologist can stake a log to the bank so it holds its position — upgrading a so-so log into prime salmon habitat. Everyone downstream benefits from the steadier, healthier creek.
Road Manager
Keeps roads, bridges, and culverts working. Threatened by debris that jams up under bridges and blocks culverts during storms.
infrastructure to stay intact
Special skill
Install a debris deflector
Only the Road Manager can fit a culvert or bridge with a deflector that catches drifting logs before they jam — the key defense against cut-and-sent wood arriving from upstream.
Land Manager
Protects property from flooding, fire, and erosion. Pays the price when upstream choices speed up and worsen flood pulses.
to prevent flooding & damage
Special skill
Open a floodplain
Only the Land Manager can set the banks back and free up floodplain, giving a flood pulse room to spread out and slow down — the one move that actively lowers flood risk instead of just bracing for it.
3 How a turn works

The game is turn-based. Players take turns one at a time, and the watershed updates as the seasons pass.

1Events happen. Logs may fall into the creek, and storms or droughts can strike — more often in the season you're in.
2The active player takes their turn. This is the only time you can take a major action (see below).
3Anyone can take minor actions. Small upkeep tasks can be done at any time, on or off your turn.
4The creek responds. Water flow, habitat, and flood risk shift based on everyone's choices — including ones made turns ago.
4 What you can do — major vs. minor

Your actions fall into two kinds. The difference is when you're allowed to do them.

Only on your turn
Major actions
Big moves that change the state of the watershed.
  • Removing a log from the creek
  • Building a well or a storage pond
  • Building or removing a channel, culvert, or bridge
  • Adding logs back in to build fish habitat
Anytime
Minor actions
Small upkeep and observation — no need to wait for your turn.
  • Repairing roads or buildings
  • Watering plants
  • Measuring water quality
  • Clearing small debris
5 The big decision — fallen wood

Logs fall into the creek on their own, and more fall after storms. Every fallen log is a choice, and it's the heart of the game. When a log lands in your reach, you decide what to do with it.

Leave it in place
A large log lying across the current carves out deep pools where salmon rest and spawn. Best for the creek.
Great for habitat
Cut it & send it downstream
Clears your own reach — but the pieces collect downstream and can jam under bridges and culverts.
Your problem becomes theirs
Remove it entirely
Opens the channel for water flow, but strips away habitat. Pools fade over the seasons that follow.
Slow cost to the creek
Facilitator notes research-grounded

Not all logs are equal, and the game classifies each one when it arrives by size and orientation. Large logs lying across the flow are the high-value ones — in coastal field studies, roughly 43% of logs over ~60 cm diameter formed pools, versus about 6% of medium (15–30 cm) logs. A log lying parallel to the current does little regardless of size.

Channel width changes the math. Fellows Creek runs ~4 m wide in the headwaters, 8–10 m mid-watershed, and 18–20 m near the estuary. A medium log can span a narrow headwater channel (acting like a large one) but can't reach across the lower reach. A useful threshold: a 20 cm log becomes meaningful at roughly 5–6 m channel width. For this watershed's logging history, treat >30–40 cm as "large," with >60 cm as a rare keystone-log event.

The key tension to surface in play: removal costs hit fast, recovery comes slow. A stripped reach loses pool structure within a few high-flow seasons, but rebuilding it after logs return takes 3–10 years. The cheap short-term choice carries a delayed price.

6 Sharing the water

The creek is one shared resource, and different pressures pull on it differently:

Drawing water to irrigate
The Farmer needs flow for crops. In wet months there's plenty to go around. In a dry season, drawing too much can leave salmon stranded.
Straightening the channel
Channeling a reach drains your own land faster — but sends a bigger, faster flood pulse to everyone downstream.
Keeping plants along the banks
A wide strip of plants beside the creek (a "buffer") filters farm runoff, shades the water, and stabilizes banks.
Protecting culverts & bridges
Debris sent downstream piles up at culverts and bridges. Stronger infrastructure resists it; storms test it.
Facilitator notes research-grounded

Irrigation & fish passage. This is a dry-season problem, not a year-round one. A working threshold for calibration: drawing more than about 20% of low-season (summer) flow starts to break pool connectivity for fish. In a drought year even a 10% draw can strand fish; in a normal year a farm might draw 30% with little impact. A storage pond (1–5 acre-feet) lets the Farmer carry through 6–8 dry weeks and can become a bargaining chip with other players.

Riparian buffers — two tiers. A narrow buffer of 10 m earns partial credit (sediment capture, some shade); a wide buffer of 20 m+ gives full credit (nutrient filtering and canopy closure too). Note the cross-role link: if the Farmer clears the bank to the creek's edge, the Ecologist loses the source material to add logs in that reach.

Channelizing. Straightened reaches can advance flood-peak timing by hours and raise peak discharge 20–40% versus a meandering reach. Upstream channelizing is paid for by the Road and Land Managers downstream.

Culverts. Most common failure is inlet blockage by debris, then capacity exceedance in storms. The Road Manager's upgrade ladder: cheap debris clearing → mid-cost inlet deflectors → expensive oversized replacement. A storm failure realistically costs $50k–$150k to repair — painful but survivable; two failures at once can be catastrophic.

Sources: Open Research Questions (sections 8–9). Specific thresholds are starting values pending expert interviews.

7 The seasons change the stakes

Turns cycle between two seasons. The same action can be safe in one and risky in the other, so plan ahead — you'll know the dry season is coming.

Wet season

Water is plentiful, so irrigation is no problem. The real threat is storms: heavy rain drops more logs and pushes flood risk up. Wood decisions matter most now.

Dry season

Flow drops low. Now the Farmer's water draw competes directly with the salmon's need to pass, and drought events can hit. The Farmer's pond investment pays off here.

9 Getting around the world

The world is built from tiles, each with its own terrain — forest, field, town, creek, and more — and each can be in different states like thriving, degraded, flooded, or dry. Your character walks the land and sees only the part of the world around them at any moment.

To see the whole watershed at once — and how your reach connects to everyone else's — open the map from the main menu. It's the best way to spot trouble heading downstream before it arrives.

10 The one thing to remember
You're all on the same creek.

Every reach flows into the next. The log you send downstream, the water you draw in a drought, the channel you straighten — each one shows up on someone else's land. Talk to each other. The watershed only thrives if you solve it together.
